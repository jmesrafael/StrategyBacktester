TRADING MATHEMATICS — SURVIVAL, RISK & EDGE
Organized Study Notes from the YouTube Transcription

PURPOSE
This video explains why a strategy can have a positive edge and still lose money. Long-term survival depends on controlling losses, position size, correlated exposure, uncertainty, and execution behavior.

The video is organized into six parts:
1. Losses and compounding
2. Streaks and randomness
3. Position size and the edge
4. Hidden risk
5. Proving the edge: edge vs. luck
6. The trading plan

============================================================
1. LOSSES AND COMPOUNDING
============================================================

1.1 Losses require disproportionately larger recoveries

A percentage loss is calculated from the current account balance. After a loss, the remaining balance is smaller, so the recovery percentage must be larger.

FORMULA — GAIN REQUIRED TO RECOVER

    Required gain = L / (1 - L)

Where:
- L = loss expressed as a decimal (e.g. 0.25 for 25%)
- Required gain = percentage gain needed to return to the starting balance

Examples:
- 10% loss: 0.10 / 0.90 = 11.11% recovery required
- 25% loss: 0.25 / 0.75 = 33.33%
- 50% loss: 0.50 / 0.50 = 100%
- 75% loss: 0.75 / 0.25 = 300%
- 90% loss: 0.90 / 0.10 = 900%

Example:
An account falls from $100 to $50 after a 50% loss. A 50% gain on $50 produces only $75. The account needs a 100% gain from $50 to return to $100.

KEY LESSON:
Protecting capital is essential. The deeper the drawdown, the harder recovery becomes.

1.2 Equal percentage wins and losses can still produce a loss

A gain and a loss of the same percentage do not cancel each other because they are applied to different balances.

Example:
    $100 × 1.10 = $110
    $110 × 0.90 = $99

One gain of 10% followed by one loss of 10% leaves the account at $99, not $100.

After ten +10%/-10% pairs:

    Final balance = $100 × (1.10 × 0.90)^10
                  ≈ $90.44

The account loses about 9.56%, despite the equal percentage gains and losses.

GENERAL COMPOUNDING FORMULA

    Final balance = Starting balance × (1 + r1) × (1 + r2) × ... × (1 + rn)

Where each r is a trade return expressed as a decimal. A loss is entered as a negative number.

1.3 Volatility and the size of swings matter

Two strategies can have the same average return but different ending balances because their gains and losses compound.

Example from the video:
- A 50% gain turns $100 into $150.
- A following 40% loss reduces $150 by $60.
- Final balance = $90.

The arithmetic average return is not enough to describe the actual compounded result. Large swings can damage capital even when the average return appears positive.

KEY LESSON:
Evaluate both expected return and volatility. Lower volatility can improve long-term compounding and make a strategy easier to survive.

1.4 A positive average does not mean every trader profits

The video uses a game that gains 50% on heads and loses 40% on tails. One heads-and-tails pair changes:

    $100 × 1.50 × 0.60 = $90

A few unusually successful participants can raise the group average while most participants lose money.

KEY LESSON:
The average outcome can be misleading when results are highly uneven. Look at the median and the distribution of individual outcomes, not only the group average.

============================================================
2. STREAKS AND RANDOMNESS
============================================================

2.1 Losing streaks are normal, even with a profitable strategy

A strategy with a 55% win rate still has a 45% chance of losing any individual trade. Random losses can cluster together.

The video gives illustrative longest losing streaks over 500 trades:
- 70% win rate: likely around 5 consecutive losses
- 50% win rate: likely around 8; a bad case around 11
- 40% win rate: a bad case can be around 11
- 30% win rate: a bad case can reach around 15

These are illustrative estimates, not guaranteed limits. Actual streaks depend on the number of trades and the sequence of outcomes.

2.2 Consecutive losses compound

When risk is a fixed percentage of the current account, each loss is taken from the reduced balance.

FORMULA — BALANCE AFTER CONSECUTIVE LOSSES

    Balance after n losses = Starting balance × (1 - r)^n

Where:
- r = fraction of current equity risked and lost per trade
- n = number of consecutive losses

Example: five consecutive losses
- At 10% risk: remaining balance = 100% × 0.90^5 ≈ 59.05%
  Account loss ≈ 40.95%.
- At 1% risk: remaining balance = 100% × 0.99^5 ≈ 95.10%
  Account loss ≈ 4.90%.

KEY LESSON:
Choose risk per trade with realistic losing streaks in mind, not just the expected win rate.

2.3 Trade order changes the path

Two traders can take the same set of winning and losing trades and finish with different results at a particular point because the sequence affects compounding and drawdown.

The video illustrates 20 random sequences of 200 trades using:
- Win rate: 55%
- Reward-to-risk ratio: 1:1
- Expected value: +0.1R per trade

Some sequences finish profitably; others finish at a loss. This does not necessarily mean the strategy's underlying edge changed. The order of wins and losses affects the path.

2.4 Short-term results are weak evidence

A short losing streak does not automatically prove that a strategy has stopped working. Likewise, a short winning streak does not prove that a strategy is profitable.

The video gives these illustrative probabilities for a 55% strategy being below its starting point:
- After 20 trades: about 25%
- After 100 trades: about 14%
- After 500 trades: about 1.1%

These figures depend on the assumptions used in the video's model. They should not be treated as universal probabilities for every strategy.

2.5 Drawdown: time spent below a previous peak

Drawdown is the decline from the highest account value reached so far.

FORMULA — DRAWDOWN

    Drawdown = (Peak equity - Current equity) / Peak equity

Example:
- Peak account value: $12,000
- Current value: $10,800
- Drawdown = ($12,000 - $10,800) / $12,000 = 10%

A profitable account can spend much of its trading history below its previous high. The video reports that about 89% of trades in its simulation occurred during periods below the latest peak. This does not mean 89% of trades were losses; it means recovery to a new high can take time.

2.6 The worst drawdown may still be ahead

The longer a strategy is traded, the more opportunities there are for an unusually bad sequence.

Illustrative worst drawdowns in the video's model:
- Around 8R after 100 trades
- Around 13R after 500 trades
- Around 20R after 2,000 trades

A backtest's maximum drawdown is only the worst drawdown observed in that sample. It is not a guaranteed maximum for future trading.

KEY LESSON:
Plan for drawdowns larger than the ones already observed.

============================================================
3. POSITION SIZE AND THE EDGE
============================================================

3.1 Position size is controllable; the next trade is not

Position size should be calculated from the amount of money you are willing to lose and the distance to the stop.

FORMULA — POSITION SIZE

    Position size = Dollar risk / Stop distance per unit

Where:
- Dollar risk = account equity × risk percentage
- Stop distance per unit = entry price minus stop price (in absolute terms)

Example:
- Account: $10,000
- Risk: 1% = $100
- Entry-to-stop distance: $2 per share
- Position size = $100 / $2 = 50 shares

A second trader using a $5 stop would buy 20 shares:

    20 shares × $5 = $100 risk

Both positions risk $100. Share quantity alone does not determine risk; stop distance matters too.

3.2 Expected value (EV) measures the average result per trade

The video uses a 55% win rate and a 1:1 reward-to-risk ratio, producing an expected value of +0.1R per trade.

FORMULA — EXPECTED VALUE

    EV = (Win probability × Average win) - (Loss probability × Average loss)

For a 55% win rate, 1R average win, and 1R average loss:

    EV = (0.55 × 1R) - (0.45 × 1R)
       = +0.10R per trade

R means the amount initially risked on a trade. If 1R equals $100, then +0.1R equals an expected +$10 per trade over a sufficiently large sample, before costs and execution effects.

3.3 Position size affects growth and survival

Increasing risk can increase expected growth while also making losses and drawdowns much more severe.

The video discusses the Kelly size: the theoretical position size that maximizes long-run compounded growth under a specified probability model and known edge.

For a simple win/loss model:

    Kelly fraction = (b × p - q) / b

Where:
- p = probability of a win
- q = probability of a loss = 1 - p
- b = net reward per unit risk (reward-to-risk ratio)

Important:
- Kelly is model-dependent and assumes the estimated probabilities and payouts are reliable.
- Full Kelly can produce very large drawdowns.
- The video recommends that traders generally use only a fraction of theoretical Kelly sizing.
- The video's example of a growth peak near 10% risk is specific to its illustration, not a universal recommendation.

3.4 Same strategy, different risk, different outcome

The video compares three accounts taking the same 300 trades:
- 165 wins and 135 losses
- Win rate: 55%
- Risk levels: 1%, 10%, and 25%

In the illustration:
- 1% risk gained about 33%.
- 10% risk gained about 349%.
- 25% risk lost about 87% of the account.

The exact results depend on the sequence and model assumptions. The point is that excessive risk can overwhelm a profitable edge.

The video also illustrates the drawdown trade-off:
- 1% risk: typical worst drawdown around 13%
- 5% risk: around 53%
- 10% risk: around 83%

These are model-specific examples, not forecasts.

3.5 Avoid doubling position size after every loss

The video describes a Martingale-style method that doubles the position after each loss, hoping one later win will recover the sequence.

Sequence of unit bets:
    1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512

Total amount lost after ten consecutive losses:

    Total = 1 + 2 + 4 + ... + 512
          = 2^10 - 1
          = 1,023 units

The method risks 1,023 units to pursue a one-unit profit. A long losing streak can create an enormous loss or exceed available capital.

KEY LESSON:
Do not assume a future win will rescue an escalating position. A fixed, preplanned risk method is safer than uncontrolled doubling.

============================================================
4. HIDDEN RISK
============================================================

A trade's visible risk includes entry, stop loss, and position size. Other risks can make the actual loss larger than planned:
- Correlation: several positions may move together.
- Gaps: price may jump past the stop.
- Fat tails: extreme market moves may occur more often than a normal distribution predicts.
- Leverage: a small market move can create a large account-level loss.

4.1 Correlation: several trades can behave like one

Correlation measures how closely two assets or strategies move together.

Correlation coefficient:
    - +1: move perfectly together
    -  0: no linear relationship
    - -1: move perfectly in opposite directions

Five positions risking 1% each do not necessarily provide five independent risks. If they are highly correlated, they may all lose together.

The video gives illustrative combined-risk figures:
- Five positions with low/no correlation: about 2.2% combined risk
- Moderate correlation: about 3.9%
- Perfectly aligned movement: up to 5%

These are model-specific illustrations. Actual portfolio risk depends on position weights, volatility, correlations, and market conditions.

KEY LESSON:
Count shared exposure, not just the number of separate trades. Five technology stocks exposed to the same interest-rate news may behave like one concentrated position.

4.2 A stop loss is an order, not a guaranteed price

A stop loss is intended to exit a position at a specified level, but it does not guarantee execution at that exact price.

Example:
- Planned loss at stop: -1R
- Market closes and reopens beyond the stop
- The order fills at the next available price
- Actual loss could become -4R

The difference between the expected execution price and actual fill is slippage.

Gaps can cause large slippage because the market may move across prices without trading at those levels.

KEY LESSON:
A stop defines an exit trigger, not a guaranteed maximum loss.

4.3 Fat tails: extreme moves are more common than a simple bell curve suggests

A normal (bell-curve) model assumes extreme outcomes are very rare. Financial returns can have fat tails, meaning unusually large moves occur more often than a normal model predicts.

The video contrasts a five-sigma move:
- Normal-model estimate: roughly once every 6,900 years
- Fat-tail illustration: roughly every 15 months

The transcript also contains another visual estimate of around three months. These are model-dependent illustrations; the exact frequency is not a universal market statistic.

KEY LESSON:
Do not build risk plans on the assumption that extreme events are practically impossible.

4.4 Leverage magnifies market movements

Leverage allows control of a position larger than the account's own capital.

Example from the video:
- Account: $10,000
- At 1:1 leverage: controls $10,000
- At 100:1 leverage: controls $1,000,000
- A 1% move against $1,000,000 = $10,000 loss
- At 500:1 leverage, a 0.2% adverse move on the $5,000,000 position equals $10,000

Leverage does not change the market movement. It magnifies the effect of that movement on account equity. Actual liquidation and margin rules depend on the broker and instrument.

5. PROVING THE EDGE: EDGE VS. LUCK
============================================================

5.1 Every result contains both edge and randomness

A strategy's expected edge may be small compared with short-term random variation.

The video gives an example of +0.1R expected value per trade:
- After 20 trades: expected edge contribution = +2R
- Random variation may be around 9R in either direction in the illustration
- After 1,000 trades: expected edge contribution = +100R

As the sample grows, the accumulated edge can become easier to distinguish from randomness. This does not remove risk or guarantee profit.

5.2 A short record can make a random trader look skilled

The video describes 100 people each flipping a fair coin 20 times. About 25% may get 12 or more heads, which looks like a 60% win rate.

But a fair coin has no edge. Some participants appear successful purely because of random variation.

KEY LESSON:
A high win rate over a small sample does not prove that a strategy has an edge.

5.3 More trades reduce uncertainty in the estimated win rate

An observed win rate is an estimate of the strategy's true win probability. With more independent observations, the estimate generally becomes more precise.

The video gives illustrative ranges:
- At 50 trades, an observed 60% win rate may still be consistent with a broad true-rate range (the transcript mentions approximately 39%–81%).
- At 500 trades, the example range narrows to approximately 56%–64%.

These ranges depend on the statistical method and assumptions. The key point is that a small sample produces substantial uncertainty.

5.4 Smaller edges need more trades to demonstrate

The video compares how long it may take for an edge to become distinguishable from noise:
- 0.2R edge: around 100 trades in the illustration
- 0.1R edge: roughly four times as many trades
- 0.05R edge: around 1,600 trades

These are illustrative model results, not universal thresholds. The relationship emphasizes that smaller edges are harder to prove and require more data.

5.5 Practical formula: expected edge over a sample

    Expected cumulative result = Number of trades × EV per trade

Example:
    200 trades × 0.1R = +20R expected result

This is an expectation, not a guaranteed outcome. Actual results can be above or below it because of randomness.

KEY LESSON:
Evaluate a strategy over a sufficiently large and representative sample. Do not declare success or failure based only on a few trades.

============================================================
6. THE TRADING PLAN: RULES THAT PROTECT THE EDGE
============================================================

6.1 Use risk limits at multiple levels

The video recommends several layers of limits:

1. Per-trade limit
   Defines how much one trade or idea is allowed to lose.

2. Daily limit
   Defines when to stop trading for the day (the video gives 3R as an example).

3. Weekly limit
   Prevents several bad trading days from becoming a larger problem.

4. Account-level limit
   Defines when to reduce position size or pause trading.

Write these rules while calm, before losses affect decision-making.

6.2 Small execution habits can turn a winning system into a losing one

The video gives an example of a system with an original expectancy of +0.2R per trade. Small changes can reduce or eliminate that edge:
- Moving the stop farther away
- Taking profits too early
- Trading costs
- Slippage

The illustration shows these changes reducing the system to approximately -0.05R per trade.

KEY LESSON:
Follow the tested rules. A strategy's real-world results can differ from its backtest when execution changes.

6.3 Reduce risk during losing periods or drawdowns

The video compares:
- Fixed risk of 2% per trade
- A method that reduces risk during losing periods

Illustrative results:
- Fixed 2% risk produced about 146% growth, compared with about 84% for the reduced-risk method.
- The bad-case drawdown was about 36% with fixed risk and 21% when reducing risk.
- The chance of a 30% drawdown was about 25% with fixed risk and near 0% with the reduced-risk method.

These are results from the video's model, not guaranteed outcomes. Reducing risk sacrifices some upside in exchange for limiting severe downside.

6.4 Core rules from the video

- Protect the downside first. A 50% loss requires a 100% gain to recover.
- Plan for losing streaks before they happen.
- Calculate position size from the dollar risk and stop distance.
- Avoid excessive position sizing, even when the strategy has an edge.
- Treat correlated positions as shared exposure.
- Remember that stops can slip and gaps can increase losses.
- Do not assume extreme market moves are impossible.
- Judge strategies using hundreds of trades and appropriate testing.
- Write trade, daily, weekly, and account-level limits in advance.
- Reduce position size when drawdowns or losing periods require it.

QUICK FORMULA REFERENCE
============================================================

1. Recovery gain after a loss

    Recovery gain = L / (1 - L)

2. Compounded account balance

    Final balance = Initial balance × (1 + r1) × ... × (1 + rn)

3. Balance after consecutive fixed-percentage losses

    Final balance = Initial balance × (1 - r)^n

4. Drawdown

    Drawdown = (Peak equity - Current equity) / Peak equity

5. Dollar risk

    Dollar risk = Account equity × Risk percentage

6. Position size

    Position size = Dollar risk / Stop distance per unit

7. Expected value

    EV = (P(win) × Average win) - (P(loss) × Average loss)

8. Kelly fraction for a simple win/loss model

    Kelly fraction = (b × p - q) / b

    p = win probability
    q = loss probability = 1 - p
    b = net reward per unit risk

9. Expected cumulative edge

    Expected cumulative result = Number of trades × EV per trade

10. Martingale total after n losing bets, starting at one unit and doubling

    Total loss = 2^n - 1

============================================================
IMPORTANT DEFINITIONS
============================================================

R:
The amount initially risked on a trade. If the planned risk is $100, then 1R = $100.

Expectancy / Expected value:
The average theoretical result per trade over many trades.

Edge:
A statistical advantage that gives a strategy positive expected value over time.

Drawdown:
The percentage decline from a previous account equity peak.

Volatility:
The degree to which returns fluctuate.

Correlation:
A measure of how closely two return series move together.

Slippage:
The difference between the expected execution price and the actual fill price.

Fat tails:
A return distribution with a higher likelihood of extreme outcomes than a normal distribution predicts.

Kelly sizing:
A theoretical method for choosing the fraction of capital to risk to maximize long-run compounded growth under specified assumptions.

============================================================
PRACTICAL STUDY ORDER
============================================================

Learn and apply these in order:

1. Understand percentage losses and recovery math.
2. Understand compounding and why equal percentage wins/losses do not cancel.
3. Calculate dollar risk and position size from the stop distance.
4. Calculate expectancy in R.
5. Understand losing streaks and drawdowns.
6. Measure combined exposure and correlation.
7. Account for gaps, slippage, fat tails, and leverage.
8. Test the strategy over a large sample.
9. Set per-trade, daily, weekly, and account-level limits.
10. Review whether live execution preserves the strategy's tested edge.

FINAL TAKEAWAY

The central message of the video is to survive first. A profitable strategy is not enough if position size is excessive, losses compound, correlated positions concentrate risk, or execution habits destroy the edge.

The goal is not simply to maximize profit on the next trade. It is to control risk well enough to remain in the market long enough for a genuine statistical edge to matter.
